Title: Abraham Lincoln’s Path to the Presidency
Subject: American Political History and Civil War Leadership
Key Figures: Abraham Lincoln, Stephen A. Douglas, Mary Todd Lincoln

Abraham Lincoln was the 16th President of the United States, steering the nation through its greatest constitutional, military, and moral crisis during the American Civil War. Born on February 12, 1809, in a humble one-room log cabin in Hardin County, Kentucky, Lincoln grew up on the impoverished frontier of Indiana and Illinois. With less than a year of formal schooling, he educated himself through voracious reading of classic literature, the Bible, and Blackstone's Commentaries on English Law.

Lincoln's political career began in New Salem, Illinois, where he won election to the Illinois State Legislature in 1834 as a member of the Whig Party. Admitted to the bar in 1836, he established a thriving legal practice in Springfield, Illinois, earning a reputation for unyielding honesty and formidable courtroom rhetoric. In 1846, Lincoln was elected to the United States House of Representatives, where he opposed the Mexican-American War and advocated gradual emancipation of enslaved persons.

The passage of the Kansas-Nebraska Act in 1854 re-ignited national conflict over the expansion of slavery and galvanized Lincoln's return to politics. Joining the newly formed Republican Party, Lincoln gained national renown during the legendary 1858 Lincoln-Douglas debates against Senator Stephen A. Douglas. Lincoln's profound moral clarity, encapsulated in his "House Divided" speech, asserted that the American government could not endure permanently half slave and half free.

In May 1860, the Republican National Convention nominated Abraham Lincoln for the presidency on a platform opposing the extension of slavery into federal territories. Winning the November 1860 presidential election, Lincoln's victory prompted southern states to secede, culminating in the Civil War. As Commander-in-Chief, President Lincoln issued the Emancipation Proclamation in 1863, delivered the immortal Gettysburg Address, and championed the Thirteenth Amendment, forever ending slavery in the United States.
